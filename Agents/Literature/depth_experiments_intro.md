## Section 1: Why Depth? Motivation for the Stereoscopic Manipulation

### Dot-Specificity and the Nature of the Cueing Effect

The delayed-onset cueing effect — the performance advantage for discriminating translation direction when the translating dot field was cued at stimulus onset — is not reducible to a simple spatial expectancy. The benefit does not arise merely from knowing which side of the display will undergo coherent motion, nor from directing attention to a broad spatial region. Rather, the cue appears to select the specific set of dots that will ultimately translate, a selection that must be spatially fine-grained and distributed across the entire aperture. This dot-specificity implies that the attentional mechanism tracks a structured, distributed object — something more like a surface representation than a spotlight or an orientation of expectancy.

This matters because it raises the question of what features define the attended surface. In the baseline paradigm, the two dot fields are distinguished only by their temporal onset asynchrony and, once both are visible, by their opposite rotation directions. The delayed-onset translation cue binds to one field, and that binding evidently persists through the 750 ms pre-translation delay and into the translation epoch itself. What exactly is being held in memory across this delay? What constitutes the attentional "handle" on the cued surface?

### Perceptual Depth Ordering of Overlapping Fields

A natural candidate emerges from introspective report: when two superimposed transparent dot fields are viewed binocularly, they tend to be perceived as occupying different depth planes — one appearing in front of the other. The reliability of this percept in the absence of binocular disparity is debatable. Without a consistent stereo signal, the perceived depth ordering of the two fields is likely driven by low-level properties such as luminance contrast or local density fluctuations, and may be unstable across trials or observers. Whether, and how consistently, any observer assigns the delayed-onset field to a particular perceived depth plane under baseline (no-stereo) conditions is not known and should ultimately be assessed empirically — for instance, by intermixing probe trials on which the observer reports perceived depth order.

The observation that perceived depth ordering occurs at all, even qualitatively, opens the following possibility: the attentional mechanism that maintains the cued-surface representation across the delay interval may be operating partly on the basis of depth-plane identity — a representation of "the nearer surface" or "the farther surface" rather than (or in addition to) "the surface rotating clockwise" or "the surface composed of specific dot tokens." Under this view, depth-plane membership would function as a grouping signal that supports coherent representation of the cued object across time.

### Depth-Plane Identity as a Component of the Attentional Object

If the mechanism of selection operates even loosely on depth-plane membership, this generates a set of specific questions. First, does introducing a reliable, manipulable stereoscopic depth signal — one that unambiguously assigns each dot field to a definite depth plane — strengthen the attentional selection, as the V1 Point-Set model would predict? Second, if depth-plane membership is a component of the surface representation, what happens when that membership is disrupted at the moment of target evaluation? The cued dots were registered as occupying, say, the far depth plane during the delay period; if they are displaced to the near depth plane precisely at the onset of translation, does the attentional pointer follow them, fail to follow them, or update to the vacated plane?

Third, and perhaps most fundamentally: the baseline experiment assigns the two fields to depth planes in a semi-arbitrary way on each trial, with no stable stereoscopic signal distinguishing them. Any depth-plane-based grouping that emerged in that setting would have had to arise spontaneously from the disparity signal itself, which is noisy and inconsistent in the absence of explicit stereo rendering. Introducing stereoscopic depth via the Meta Quest 3 makes the depth planes explicit, stable, and parametrically variable — allowing a direct test of whether depth-plane membership modulates the cueing effect and, if so, how.

### How the Depth Experiments Address These Questions

Introducing stereoscopic depth into the paradigm made the depth planes fully controllable. The DepthParam experiment family established a parametric baseline: at all tested separations (0.03–0.15 m at 2 m viewing distance), an overall cueing advantage is present. However, the aggregate advantage masks a striking and consistent asymmetry: cueing is strong and robust for dots translating in the far depth plane, while cueing for near-plane translators is present but substantially weaker at all tested separations, and grows weaker as depth separation increases. This Far > Near asymmetry — holding translation depth constant and comparing CUED to UNCUED — is positive in sign throughout and does not reverse at any tested separation, reaching significance in the largest combined dataset (DepthColorLinked, n=512, +8.6pp*). An earlier framing of this asymmetry as a "near-plane cueing reversal" was a labeling artifact: it arose from comparing CUED Near to UNCUED Near across different translation depths, a confounded contrast. The corrected analysis holds translation depth constant and finds CUED > UNCUED throughout. The Far > Near asymmetry is not predicted by any existing 2D surface-attention account and suggests that depth-plane identity interacts with the cueing mechanism asymmetrically depending on which plane is attended.

The DepthSwapCtrl and DepthColorLinked experiments then directly manipulated depth-plane continuity at the moment of translation onset. The ZdA swap condition disrupted depth continuity for the coherent (cued) translating object; the ZdB swap condition preserved the cued object's depth-plane identity while displacing the background incoherent dots. Both experiments showed a selective disruption when the cued object's depth identity was severed (ZdA/ZdCoh conditions), and no disruption — or even enhancement — when the background dots were displaced instead (ZdB/ZdNoi). Critically, in DepthColorLinked, ZdNoi and ZdCoh are perfectly matched for the total amount of depth change in the scene (50% of dots in both conditions); the only difference is *which* dots change. The UNCUED arm is essentially flat across conditions throughout. This rules out any general scene-disruption account and localizes the effect specifically to the attentional object's depth-plane continuity. The DecoupledDots experiment further disentangled color from depth by allowing them to swap independently, and found that color changes alone (swap type C) had no effect on cueing while depth changes alone (swap type Z) reproduced the disruption. The operative variable is depth-plane identity, not color identity.

Together, these findings establish that depth-plane membership is a constitutive feature of the attentional object in the VRDots paradigm — not merely a contextual label, but a property whose continuity is required for the attentional pointer to remain anchored to the translating surface.

## Section 2: Open Questions Posed by These Results

The experiments described above establish the existence and robustness of depth-plane effects on surface-based attentional selection. They also generate a set of specific, answerable empirical questions that define the next phase of inquiry.

### 2.1 Disparity Specificity versus Depth-Order Generality

The DepthParam results reveal a clear and monotonic pattern across the four depth separations tested (0.03–0.15 m): Near-plane cueing is positive but weak at 0.03 m (+12.5 pp, n.s.) and weaker still at larger separations, while Far-plane cueing is large and essentially constant at all tested depths. Assessed correctly — holding translation depth constant and comparing CUED to UNCUED at each depth — cueing is positive for both Near and Far throughout; the Far > Near gap grows with depth separation. The DepthParam data at n=32/cell are too sparse to characterize this growth quantitatively with confidence; second sessions at each depth are required before any functional form can be fit.

The question of what drives the growing Far > Near gap as disparity increases has two distinct answers that must be dissociated. A disparity-detection threshold account would predict that near-plane dots fail to be perceived as depth-separated below some critical disparity, at which point depth-plane-based selection collapses for Near but not Far — a step-function prediction. A continuous disparity-scaling account would predict that the Far > Near gap grows smoothly with depth separation, tracking the increasing gradient strength or neural selectivity across the entire range. A parametric disparity sweep at finer resolution around the 0.03–0.05 m range (e.g., 0.033, 0.037, 0.041, 0.045 m) would determine which functional form better describes the data and whether the onset of the Far > Near gap tracks the observer's stereo threshold for near versus far disparity at 2 m.

### 2.2 XY versus Z Tracking when Depth Identity Is Disrupted

When the cued dots change depth plane at tStart (ZdA/ZdCoh), performance drops substantially but does not fall to chance. In the DecoupledDots Z-swap condition, for example, a significant cueing advantage persists (CUED+Z: +9.0 pp **). This residual cueing could reflect at least two mechanisms: (a) the observer partially tracks the cued dots by their retinotopic (XY) position, which is preserved even when depth changes; or (b) the observer maintains a weakened or partial surface representation that survives the depth-plane discontinuity. These accounts predict different patterns under a manipulation that decouples XY and Z changes. If a lateral positional offset were introduced simultaneously with the depth swap (so that the cued dots move in both depth and XY space at tStart), a tracking-by-XY account predicts an additive disruption, whereas a weakened-representation account predicts no further cost beyond the depth swap alone. Designing a stimulus that independently varies the magnitude of the XY offset and the depth swap would allow these to be disentangled.

### 2.3 Monocular versus Binocular Locus of the Depth Effects

Monocular viewing sessions (eyes occluded individually) revealed that the dot-cueing effect (F1) survives monocularly, though attenuated (+7–10 pp, p < .05, versus +19.8 pp binocularly). By contrast, the Near/Far asymmetry collapses entirely under monocular viewing (binocular: +36.8 pp interaction z = 6.50; monocular: +10.7 pp, marginal). This dissociation implies two separable components: a monocular component of surface-based selection (presumably based on motion coherence and temporal onset, operating on luminance-contrast signals) and a binocular component tied to disparity-defined depth-plane identity. The depth-field cueing effect (F2) shows a more ambiguous pattern monocularly — significant at marginal levels in the pooled monocular data but inconsistent across individual sessions and eye conditions, complicated by an asymmetry between left- and right-eye monocular performance (likely due to floaters in the right eye). A clean parametric characterization of the monocular residuals — with better-controlled eye conditions, larger n per session, and preferably a second observer — would clarify whether any component of the depth-field cueing effect is truly monocular or whether it is entirely binocular in origin.

### 2.4 Disparity-Tuned Neurons versus Perceived Depth Order

The depth effects observed in VRDots could originate at early cortical stages — in V1/V2 disparity-tuned neurons that represent absolute retinal disparity — or at later stages that represent perceived depth order as a figural or surface-level property. These two accounts make distinct predictions. The early account predicts that the depth-field cueing effect (F2) and the Near/Far asymmetry should scale with absolute disparity magnitude in a way that tracks known disparity-tuning functions in V1/V2 (approximately monotone increasing over the range tested, with possible saturation at large disparities). The late account predicts that the effects should track perceived depth segregation quality — a threshold function that is more categorical than monotone. The parametric disparity sweep (Section 2.1) would begin to address this, but the most direct test would be an ERP or MEG study examining the latency of depth-selective modulation. If depth-plane identity modulates the C1 component (onset latency ~50–80 ms), the locus is in V1/V2; if modulation appears only at N1 or later latencies, it reflects higher-area processing. A further behavioral test: manipulating perceived depth order without changing retinal disparity (e.g., by adding monocular occlusion cues that reverse the figure-ground assignment of the two planes) should modulate the cueing effect if the late account is correct, and should have no effect if the early account is correct.

### 2.5 The Role of Color: Chromatic Salience or Depth-Plane Tagging?

The DepthColorLinked experiment introduced red and green as consistent markers of the near and far depth planes, making color and depth covary. The DecoupledDots experiment subsequently showed that color changes alone (swap type C) have essentially no effect on the cueing magnitude (CUED+N: +23.5 pp versus CUED+C: +23.5 pp), while depth changes (swap type Z and CZ) approximately halve the effect. Color is therefore not a necessary component of surface-based selection, consistent with Mitchell, Stoner, Fallah and Reynolds (2003) who established the same point in the 2D paradigm. Nevertheless, there is a persistent numerical observation across sessions that overall performance is somewhat elevated with distinct colors (R/G) relative to all-red conditions. Whether this reflects a modest facilitative role of color in surface segregation, an idiosyncratic observer sensitivity to chromatic contrast, or a response-stage bias for particular hue-direction pairings cannot be determined from the current data. A controlled experiment with isoluminant color pairs, counterbalanced across observers, would clarify whether the apparent advantage is chromatic salience, depth-plane tagging, or observer-specific.

### 2.6 The Minimum Temporal Signal for Depth-Plane-Based Selection

The depth experiments reveal a consistent asymmetry between the CUED and UNCUED arms: depth-plane continuity benefits only the CUED observer and provides essentially no advantage in the UNCUED arm. Across all depth experiments, UNCUED performance is near chance and flat across depth conditions (e.g., DepthColorLinked: UNCUED+ZdNoi = 21.9%, UNCUED+ZdCoh = 23.4%; DepthSwapCtrl: UNCUED+N and UNCUED+ZdA and UNCUED+ZdB all in the 31–41% range). This means that depth-plane identity, in isolation, is not sufficient for attentional selection — it must be bound to a temporal event (the delayed-onset translation cue) to yield a performance advantage. The depth information is available throughout the trial, yet it confers no benefit without the onset cue. What is the minimum temporal signal needed to activate depth-plane-based selection? A stimulus-onset asynchrony (SOA) manipulation — varying the delay between field onset and translation across a range from 0 to 1000 ms — would probe the temporal dynamics of depth-plane binding: how quickly depth-plane membership is incorporated into the surface representation, and whether the strength of the F1×F2 conjunction (which in current experiments is measured at 750 ms SOA) varies systematically with cue duration.

### 2.7 Generalization beyond a Single Observer

All data reported here are from a single observer (GS). Session-to-session variability is large at the n values currently available — the individual sessions in DepthColorLinked show F2 Depth Cueing ranging from +12.5 pp (p < .05) in session 1 to +1.6 pp (n.s.) in session 2, with similar variance in DepthSwapCtrl. The central effects — F1 dot cueing and the F1×F2 conjunction — are highly consistent and statistically robust when pooled across sessions, but secondary findings (Near/Far asymmetry, ZdB enhancement) have not been replicated in a second observer. Recruiting a second observer at the key conditions (0.05 m DepthSwapCtrl binocular, and one or two DepthParam depths) is a prerequisite for any generalization claim and should be a near-term priority before extending the parametric sweep.

### 2.8 Attentional Gradient versus Neural Cross-Talk: The Three-Plane Experiment

Two mechanistic accounts of the Far > Near cueing asymmetry are currently viable and make divergent predictions (see also `depth_lit_review.md`, Section 7). The neural cross-talk account (Calabro & Vaina 2011) attributes the asymmetry to an anisotropy in MT's disparity-tuned population: more neurons prefer near (crossed) disparities, producing greater cross-talk between the two surfaces' MT representations when the near surface is attended. The attentional topology account (proposed by the experimenter GS on the basis of introspective observation during data collection) attributes the asymmetry to a structural property of the display: in a two-plane display, attention directed to Far has no plane beyond it into which the attentional gradient can bleed, whereas attention directed to Near always bleeds toward Far. The Far advantage is therefore a consequence of the boundary of the display, not of neural population structure.

These accounts make opposite predictions for a three-plane display (Near / Mid / Far). Under the neural cross-talk account, the Far advantage over Mid should replicate the Near advantage pattern, because the mechanism is determined by absolute disparity tuning: Mid is closer to fixation than Far, so the same MT-anisotropy logic predicts Far > Mid, independent of whether a plane exists beyond Far. Under the attentional topology account, the Far advantage over Mid should diminish substantially — or disappear — when a plane is added beyond Far, because Far is no longer the outermost plane and its attentional gradient now bleeds into the beyond-Far region. The critical prediction is that the Far advantage observed in the two-plane display is a *relative* rather than absolute property — it depends on Far being the outermost available surface, not on Far disparity per se.

The most informative within-subjects design compares: (a) a two-plane display with Near and Far planes, and (b) a three-plane display with Near, Mid, and an additional Beyond-Far plane. In condition (b), the former Far plane is now the Mid plane. The neural cross-talk account predicts that the cueing asymmetry for Mid vs. Near in condition (b) approximates the Far vs. Near asymmetry in condition (a) — because the absolute disparity of Mid equals the former Far, and MT-anisotropy is disparity-based. The attentional topology account predicts that Mid's cueing advantage over Near is substantially reduced in condition (b) relative to condition (a), because Mid is no longer the outermost plane. The experiment requires care in equating overall task difficulty and ensuring observers can segment three depth planes, but is technically feasible in the VRDots paradigm by adding a third disparity offset layer.

### 2.9 Does Spontaneous Depth-Order Assignment Contribute to Baseline Cueing?

*Speculative/theoretical — not yet empirically tested.*

In zero-disparity (baseline) VRDots conditions, the two rotating dot fields are depth-order ambiguous: no stereoscopic signal distinguishes which is nearer. The hypothesis proposed here is that the delayed-onset field tends to be spontaneously perceived as the nearer, figure-like surface for two converging reasons. First, Chopin & Mamassian (2011) showed that task-relevant and attended surfaces in transparent motion rivalry are more frequently reported as "in front" — and the cued field in VRDots is precisely the task-relevant surface from trial onset. Second, classical figure-ground principles assign abrupt-onset objects the role of figure against the continuous ground (the always-present non-delayed field), and figures are phenomenologically in front. If spontaneous depth-order assignment consistently places "near" on the delayed field, then the baseline F1 cueing effect (CUED > UNCUED) may be receiving a parallel, depth-order-mediated boost in addition to the temporal onset mechanism.

This hypothesis is partially consistent with F1 but is insufficient to account for the full result pattern. The Far > Near cueing asymmetry in binocular depth conditions contradicts a central prediction: if the delayed field is spontaneously near regardless of stereo assignment, Far-delayed trials should suffer a conflict cost, reducing Far performance. The opposite is observed — Far cueing is consistently and substantially stronger than Near cueing. This implies that stereoscopic disparity immediately overrides the spontaneous depth-order bias (consistent with Nakayama, Shimojo & Silverman, 1989), and that the Far > Near asymmetry originates from a mechanism — MT cross-talk anisotropy or attentional topology — that is independent of figural/spontaneous depth-order perception. Stronger still, the flatness of the UNCUED arm across all depth conditions rules out any depth-order account, spontaneous or stereo-derived, as sufficient to drive cueing: depth-plane identity cannot produce a selection advantage without the F1 temporal onset signal. The onset cue is necessary, not merely one of several sufficient routes.

The direct empirical test is measurement of spontaneous depth-order perception in baseline sessions via intermixed probe trials — the observer reports which field appears in front on probe trials, and these reports are correlated with the cueing magnitude across observers and sessions. A positive, reliable correlation would support the hypothesis that spontaneous depth-order assignment contributes to baseline (zero-disparity) F1; its absence would argue that F1 is driven entirely by temporal onset tagging, independent of any depth-order percept.
